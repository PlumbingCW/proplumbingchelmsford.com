Is Hydro Jetting Safe for Old Pipes in Chelmsford, MA?

What hydro jetting does, which old pipes can take it, and why a camera look comes first.

Call (866) 310-6010

What hydro jetting does

Hydro jetting sends water through the sewer line at high pressure from a nozzle that pulls itself forward. The water scours grease, scale, sludge and roots off the pipe wall. A cable machine punches a hole through a clog. A jet cleans the pipe itself.

That is why people ask whether it is safe for old pipes. The honest answer is that it depends on the pipe. Sound pipes handle it well. Cracked or collapsing pipes can be damaged. Our hydro jetting page covers the service, and this guide covers the old-pipe question.

Which old pipes handle jetting

PipeConditionJetting.
Cast ironSound with heavy scaleUsually fine.
Cast ironFlaking or thin wallUse caution or avoid.
ClayIntact jointsFine at moderate pressure.
ClayCracked or offset jointsAvoid.
PVC or ABSAnyFine.
Orangeburg (fiber)AnyAvoid, replace instead.

The real question is the condition of the pipe, not its age. A camera inspection answers it.

Why we look first

Before we jet an older line we run a camera through it. The video shows cracks, offsets, roots and wall thickness. If the pipe is damaged, jetting could push water into the soil and wash out the ground around the break. In that case we recommend repair, not more pressure.

This is why an inspection often comes before the jet. It also gives you a record to compare against after the cleaning.

Jetting versus snaking

  • A cable cuts a hole through a clog and often leaves residue behind.
  • A jet clears the full pipe wall and removes the buildup that causes repeats.
  • Jetting costs more per visit but can reduce repeat calls.
  • A cable is fine for a simple, fresh clog in a sound pipe.

For a one-off sink clog, drain cleaning with a cable is enough. For a main line that keeps blocking, jetting is often the better choice.

What can go wrong and how it is avoided

The main risks are a pipe that was already broken, a nozzle that lodges at an offset joint and water pushed back into the house through open fixtures. We set pressure to the pipe, use the right nozzle, cap or close low fixtures and watch the cleanout during the run.

None of this is exotic. It is the routine care a trained operator takes on every job.

What the process looks like step by step

  1. Camera inspectionWe look at the pipe first and mark any defects.
  2. Access and setupWe open the cleanout and set up the jetter and hose.
  3. Pressure set to the pipeWe start low and raise only as needed.
  4. Jetting passThe nozzle pulls forward, scouring the walls, then back out with debris.
  5. Final camera passWe confirm the line is clean and record any damage found.

The two camera passes protect you. The first tells us whether jetting is safe, and the second shows what the cleaning accomplished.

Signs that a line should not be jetted

  • Visible cracks, holes or missing pieces of pipe.
  • A section that is collapsed or severely bellied.
  • Orangeburg or fiber conduit of any age.
  • Cast iron that is flaking or paper thin.
  • Joints that have separated or shifted.

In those cases we recommend a repair or relining first. A cleaning is not worth the risk of washing out soil around a break.

Aftercare for a freshly jetted line

A clean line stays clean longer when you change the habits that fouled it. Keep grease out of the kitchen drain, avoid flushing anything but toilet paper and consider an annual check if the line has a history of problems. Roots grow back where they entered, so a line with root intrusion may need a periodic cut or a repair at the joint.

If the pipe was jetted and the problem returned within weeks, ask for the camera video. It usually shows why.
