A neighborhood that grew street by street

The Westlands was once Precinct 6 of Chelmsford and was developed mainly in the 1920s. The Chelmsford Historical Society says it never had a village center, but it had a school and an active improvement association, and many of its early residents moved from Lowell.

Because it grew over many decades, the Westlands has houses from several periods on modest lots. This page covers what we could verify, how to treat the housing numbers that listing sites publish, and the plumbing questions that suit a mixed-age neighborhood.

What we could verify about the Westlands

  • The Chelmsford Historical Society describes the Westlands as the former Precinct 6, mainly developed in the 1920s, with no village center.
  • The 1980 state survey places the Westlands side streets off Steadman Street and Chelmsford Road, and describes modest side-hall houses and cottages there.
  • The town master plan names the Westlands among the parts of town where single-family homes are most prevalent.
  • It borders Lowell, so some calls cross the line. See our Lowell plumber page for that side.

How to read the housing numbers

Listing sites describe the Westlands as mostly single-family houses, including raised ranches, Cape Cods and Garrison-style homes, with a median year built near 1955 and a median lot near 14,800 square feet. These are estimates from a real estate site, not town records, and they cover the whole neighborhood. A single street can differ a lot.

The practical use of the numbers is modest. They suggest houses built across several decades, so one house may have its original plumbing and the next may have been renovated. We do not assume which. We ask the age of the house and the water heater on every call.

Water heaters in a mixed-age neighborhood

A tank water heater has a label with a serial number that shows when it was made. Watch a unit that is more than a decade old for rust at the base and a wet pan. If it leaks from the tank itself, it needs replacing, and water heater replacement explains the job and the permit.

If the unit is newer and has simply stopped heating, repair is the first question, and an anode rod check is one inexpensive step that can extend a tank's life.

Basements, drains and the first questions

  • Basement water after heavy rain: check the sump pump first, then see sump pump service.
  • A slow drain at one fixture: drain cleaning.
  • A toilet that runs or rocks: toilet repair.
  • Sewer or septic: the Sewer Division at 978-250-5233 keeps as-built plans for most connections.

Planning an addition or a change of use

The town has a sewer capacity and connection rule, in effect since November 1, 2022. The Department of Public Works found that development and changes in how a property is used could push sewer flow above the town's permitted capacity with the City of Lowell. That is why the rule exists.

If you plan an addition, a new bathroom or a change of use on a sewered property, ask the Sewer Division whether the rule affects you before you hire anyone. The plumbing side of the work, including new fixtures and supply lines, follows once that answer is in hand.

What we could not confirm

We could not confirm a published boundary for the Westlands, so we use the Steadman Street and Chelmsford Road side streets named in the state survey as anchors. We could not confirm which streets are sewered or how old the plumbing is on any one street. We found no evidence that plumbing problems are more common here than elsewhere, and this page does not claim it.

Plumbing work in Massachusetts follows the state plumbing code, 248 CMR. The neighborhood history comes from the Chelmsford Historical Society and the Massachusetts Historical Commission's 1980 reconnaissance survey, and the sewer rule is the town's Sewer Capacity and Connection Restriction, Division III.
